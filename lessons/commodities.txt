## The big idea

A **commodity** is a basic raw material that is much the same wherever it comes from: crude oil, gold, silver, copper, wheat, cotton, natural gas. One barrel of a given grade of oil is as good as another, so commodities are bought and sold in huge amounts at prices set by worldwide demand and supply.

These prices matter because commodities sit at the start of almost everything we buy. Oil becomes petrol, diesel, plastic and fertiliser. Copper goes into wires and fans. Wheat becomes atta. When a raw material gets costlier, the things made from it often follow, usually with a delay.

## An everyday analogy

Think of a dhaba. If the price of cooking gas and onions jumps, the owner can either raise the price of a thali or earn less on each one. Companies face the same choice when commodities rise. Some pass the cost on to customers; others swallow it and their profits shrink.

## A real Indian example

India imports most of the crude oil it uses and pays for it in dollars. When the international price of **Brent crude** rises, India's import bill grows, more dollars are needed, and the rupee tends to weaken. Costlier fuel also raises transport costs, which can push up the price of vegetables, milk and other goods. That's why oil is often the biggest single worry for India's inflation.

Gold is different. Indian families buy it for weddings, festivals and savings, and most of it is also imported. Many people see gold as a safe store of value, so its price often rises when the world feels uncertain, such as during wars or market crashes. Jewellery shops in India set their prices from the international gold price, converted into rupees, plus import duty, GST and making charges. Tax rates on gold change from time to time.

## Common misunderstandings

- **"When oil rises, petrol prices rise the next day."** Not always. In India, fuel prices are partly shaped by government taxes and oil company decisions, so changes can be delayed or softened.
- **"Gold always goes up."** Gold can fall for long stretches. It tends to do well in uncertain times but pays no interest or dividend.
- **"Commodity prices only matter to traders."** They reach your kitchen, your fuel tank and your EMI, because higher inflation can lead the RBI to keep interest rates high.

## How it shows up in the news

You'll read lines like "Brent crude fell 2% to $104 a barrel" or "gold futures on MCX rose to ₹1,47,850 per 10 grams". **MCX** is India's main commodity exchange, where gold, silver, oil and metals are traded. Reports usually link price moves to wars, supply cuts by oil-producing countries, shipping problems, the strength of the US dollar or worries about the world economy. Share prices react too: costlier oil can hurt paint, airline and tyre companies, while helping oil producers.

## Three things to remember

1. Commodities are raw materials whose prices are set worldwide and flow into everyday prices.
2. Because India imports most of its oil and gold, their prices also affect the rupee.
3. Gold is often bought for safety in uncertain times, but it can fall too.
